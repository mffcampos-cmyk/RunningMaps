# Technical architecture, methodology and cost of an activity-based neighbourhood "attractiveness for living" web map (as of October 2026)

Scope note: These notes cover methodology (activity-intensity normalisation and Strava validation/bias), composite liveability scoring, front-end/tile stack and costs, back-end pipeline, comparable open-source projects, privacy/legal design, and business sustainability. Adjacent topics (e.g. data licensing of Strava Metro itself, specific national census APIs) were left to other researchers. Where a fact comes from a search-engine summary of a page I could not open directly, this is flagged as "(not opened directly)".

## 1. Methodology: normalising activity counts into a comparable "activity intensity" index, and how well Strava counts match reality

### Takeaway
Strava counts correlate moderately-to-strongly with physical counters at monthly/aggregate scales (R ≈ 0.7–0.9, R² up to 0.9) when Strava's share of total traffic is above roughly 7%, but correlation collapses at low Strava penetration (R = 0.42 in Vancouver 2019 at 5.7% share), at daily/weekly scales, in winter, and for walkers/runners versus cyclists. Published bias-correction practice is to regress Strava counts on ground-truth counts plus census covariates (income, % white, traffic speed, land use) rather than to use raw per-capita normalisation.

### Cited Findings

**Validation against physical counters**
- Fischer, Nelson & Winters (2022), *Findings*: hourly Strava counts matched to 19 permanent counters in Vancouver and 29 counters in Victoria (BC), April–October 2019 and 2020. Correlation (R) Strava vs counters: Vancouver 0.42 (2019) → 0.73 (2020); Victoria 0.85 (2019) → 0.87 (2020). Women-only Strava data gave R = 0.79 in Vancouver 2020; adults 55+ gave R = 0.88 in Victoria 2020. — [Fischer et al. 2022, Findings](https://findingspress.org/article/33280)
- Same paper proposes a benchmark Strava "usage rate" of ≥ ~7% of total bicycle volumes for cities wanting reliable Strava data; derived observationally (Victoria 7.0% share ↔ R = 0.85; Vancouver 5.7% ↔ R = 0.42; Vancouver 12.1% ↔ R = 0.73). The authors explicitly call the ≥0.7 correspondence "possible" and note representativeness is dynamic (lockdowns, weather, app uptake). — [Fischer et al. 2022](https://findingspress.org/article/33280)
- Roy, Nelson, Fotheringham & Winters (2019), *Urban Science*: 2016 Strava vs official counts at 44 locations in Maricopa County, AZ (training) and 60 in Tempe (test); Strava and official counts correlated at R² = 0.76; after LASSO + Poisson bias correction, 86% of Tempe road segments were predicted within ±100 average annual daily bicyclists. — [MDPI Urban Science 3(2):62](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/3/2/62/htm); code at [github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction](https://github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction)
- Venter et al. (2023), *Landscape and Urban Planning*, Oslo: Strava matched observed recreational spatial patterns well (R² = 0.9 at monthly scale in summer) but precision dropped at weekly and daily scales and in winter; Strava's share of total recreationist activity rose from 2.5% (2016) to 5.7% (2020); users skewed toward cyclists, males and middle-aged people; surveyed pedestrians skewed to higher income and education. — [NINA Brage record](https://brage.nina.no/nina-xmlui/handle/11250/3091374) (not opened directly; summarised via search); article page [ScienceDirect S0169204623000051](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169204623000051) returned 403.
- Ottawa: a study using Strava Metro 2016 one-minute-resolution data over a 20 km² downtown area reported correlation coefficients between daily Strava totals and automated counters of 0.76–0.96. — reported in [Findings article 10828](https://findingspress.org/article/10828) (not opened directly; attribution to this specific URL is uncertain, see Gaps)
- Lee & Sener (2021), "Strava Metro data for bicycle monitoring: a literature review", *Transport Reviews* 41(1):27–47, DOI 10.1080/01441647.2020.1798558: concludes Strava Metro can support demand estimation and exposure control in crash models, but "under-representativeness of the general population, bias towards and away from certain groups, and lack of demographic and trip details at the individual level limit reliance on it alone." — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/transr/v41y2021i1p27-47.html); PDF mirror [observatoriodabicicleta.org.br](https://observatoriodabicicleta.org.br/uploads/2023/03/Strava-Metro-bicycle-monitoring-literature-review-Kyuhyun-Lee-Ipek-Nese-Sener.pdf)
- A 2026 arXiv paper ("A global framework to estimate urban spatial cycling patterns based on crowdsourced data") notes that mismatches between Strava-derived volumes and counts "may arise not only from spatial and other biases in the Strava data, but also from spatial and other biases in the count data." — [arXiv 2603.00095](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00095)
- Brussels (2026): researchers trained a neural network on the relationship between Strava edge activity and automated counts, adding weather and day-of-week features, to estimate daily volumes. — [ScienceDirect S2667091726000282](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667091726000282) (not opened directly)
- University of Maine: calibrated Strava-based adjustment factors outperform uncalibrated factors in predicting short-duration counts. — [UMaine Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.library.umaine.edu/mcspc_transport/19) (not opened directly)
- Visitor-monitoring conference (IMC 2025): "High correlation between STRAVA data and field measurements was confirmed in case of cycling (MTB) and walking / running activities. However, more digital traces of bikers in comparison to hikers / joggers were available at STRAVA Metro platform." — [imc2025.info](https://imc2025.info/?p=40573) (not opened directly)

**Demographic bias and census-based correction / equity**
- Strava data historically under-represent women and older adults (Griffin & Jiao 2019, as cited); race and SES are not collected by Strava but app users are believed to be predominantly White and more affluent. — [Fischer et al. 2022](https://findingspress.org/article/33280)
- Roy et al. (2019) found the significant bias-correction covariates were: proportion of white population, median household income, traffic speed, distance to residential areas, and distance to green spaces — i.e. census tract demographics are used as regressors, not as a simple per-capita denominator. — [MDPI Urban Science](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/3/2/62/htm)
- Nelson et al. (2021), *Transportation Research Part C*, generalised the Strava-to-all-cyclists model across cities and found model accuracy was influenced most by the official count data used for training. — [UVic DSpace](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/abfc4340-7289-4ef4-986c-33b271300dc1) (not opened directly)
- A Transport Findings study of Victoria, BC found Strava's "commute" label aligns better with overall ridership than recreational trips, and recreational ridership was spatially higher in areas with older populations. — [Findings 16710](https://findingspress.org/article/16710)
- "Data visibility of cyclists: social justice implications of Strava Metro data in transport planning" (*Mobilities*, 2025, DOI 10.1080/17450101.2025.2542163): UK/US transport professionals view Strava Metro as statistically valid and matching counters, yet demographically skewed toward younger men, which "distorts understandings of cycling" and may slow efforts toward diverse participation. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17450101.2025.2542163); TRID record [trid.trb.org/View/2703964](https://trid.trb.org/View/2703964)
- Interviews at Transport for London and Oregon DOT: using Strava Metro to evaluate policy "unfairly privilege[s] people who are already highly mobile, and who may cycle out of choice, not necessity." — [Brighton doctoral thesis "Datafication of cycling"](https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/datafication-of-cycling/)

**Effect of Strava's own privacy binning on usable counts**
- Raturi, Hong, McArthur & Livingston (2021), *Journal of Transport Geography* 92, 103020: applied Strava's post-July-2018 binning/discretisation to original 2013–2016 Glasgow data at hourly/daily/monthly levels, compared with manual counts and spatial autocorrelation statistics; "Our results confirmed significant amount of information loss" and "conclusions reached by previous studies could have been reversed" under the binned format. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jotrge/v92y2021ics0966692321000739.html)
- Strava Metro aggregates activities "only if there were at least three unique users (a data privacy measure) during the given time window", and a UK practitioner quoted in another paper describes counts being rolled into multiples of 5. — reported by search engine as from an academic paper (most likely Venter et al. 2023, [ScienceDirect S0169204623000051](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169204623000051)) and the *Mobilities* 2025 paper; neither opened directly, see Gaps.
- Strava's own Metro help centre says only that data is "aggregated and de-identified", that activities shared with everyone or followers are included, and private activities and deleted accounts are excluded; it does not publish a numeric threshold. — [Strava Metro help: How Strava activities contribute to Strava Metro data](https://stravametro.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360045856414-How-Strava-activities-contribute-to-Strava-Metro-data); Metro FAQ states data is aggregated and de-identified to comply with GDPR and CCPA — [metro.strava.com/faq](https://metro.strava.com/faq) (not opened directly)
- Strava Metro's aggregation unit is the street network: data is "anonymize[d] and aggregate[d] ... onto a GIS of the street network" (edge/segment counts per time window), i.e. route-segment aggregation rather than hex grids. — [Bath Hacked project description](https://www.bathhacked.org/projects/bath-cycling/strava-metro-can-tell-us-cycling-bath)

**Grid/hex aggregation practice (technical, not Strava-specific)**
- DuckDB's H3 community extension example: convert lat/lng to H3 cells at resolution 9, `GROUP BY` cell to count points per hex, and suppress low-count cells with a `HAVING` clause — a direct pattern for a minimum-count threshold. — [DuckDB community extensions post, July 2024](https://duckdb.org/2024/07/05/community-extensions)
- Walk Score computes city rankings on a grid of points about 500 ft apart, weighted by population density (a precedent for population-weighting point-grid scores). — [Walk Score methodology](https://www.walkscore.com/methodology.shtml)

### Inferences
- A defensible "activity intensity" index for a liveability map should (a) aggregate to street segments or H3 hexes with a minimum-unique-contributor threshold (Strava uses ≥3 users per segment per window and "multiple users" for the heatmap), (b) normalise not by resident population alone but by a modelled expected count that controls for Strava's known covariates (income, ethnicity, age, land use), and (c) present the result as a relative percentile within the city rather than as absolute trip counts, because absolute Strava counts are only reliable where penetration is ≥ ~7%.
- Because walkers/runners are under-captured relative to cyclists (Oslo; IMC 2025) and Metro rounds counts to multiples of 5, a "neighbourhood attractiveness" layer built on running/walking heat will systematically under-score low-density, older, lower-income neighbourhoods unless corrected — exactly the equity failure mode documented in the *Mobilities* 2025 paper.
- Monthly or annual aggregation is the only temporal scale at which Strava has been shown to be precise (R² 0.9 monthly vs poor daily/weekly in Oslo), so a liveability map should not advertise time-of-day or day-of-week breakdowns at neighbourhood scale without counter calibration.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full text of Venter et al. 2023 or the *Mobilities* 2025 paper to confirm the exact wording of the "at least three unique users" and "multiples of 5" rules; the Strava Metro help centre and FAQ pages I could reach do not state a number. The "3 or more" rule is therefore well-attested in secondary academic sources but not verified against a primary Strava document.
- Lee & Sener's review almost certainly tabulates correlation coefficients across studies, but I could not access the full text; only the qualitative conclusion is cited.
- The Ottawa 0.76–0.96 figure came from a search summary and I could not confirm which URL contains it.
- I found no published study that validates Strava *running/walking* counts against pedestrian counters in urban (as opposed to trail/recreational) settings with reported coefficients.

## 2. Composite liveability scoring: how Walk Score, Bike Score, 15-minute-city indices, Healthy Streets and academic liveability indices weight components, and transparency best practice

### Takeaway
Published composite indices converge on simple, explainable aggregation: Walk Score uses distance-decayed amenity points (max within 0.25 mi / 5 min, zero after 30 min) plus density/connectivity penalties with proprietary weights; Bike Score uses four equally weighted (25% each) components; the Australian Urban Liveability Index uses unweighted indicators normalised to mean 100/SD 10 and penalises imbalance; the Sony CSL 15-minute-city tool ranks ~10,000 cities by "proximity time" on foot or by bike. Equal weights plus a published formula, with the components exposed, is the de facto transparency standard; the strongest documented critique of these indices is that they ignore socioeconomic equity.

### Cited Findings

**Walk Score / Bike Score / Transit Score**
- Walk Score: "Points are awarded based on the distance to amenities in each category. Amenities within a 5 minute walk (.25 miles) are given maximum points. A decay function is used to give points to more distant amenities, with no points given after a 30 minute walk." Pedestrian friendliness is measured via population density, block length and intersection density. Data sources: Google, Factual, Great Schools, OpenStreetMap, U.S. Census, Localeze, user-added places. Bands: 90–100 Walker's Paradise, 70–89 Very Walkable, 50–69 Somewhat Walkable, 25–49 and 0–24 Car-Dependent. The exact amenity weights and decay constants are not published (the method is patented). — [walkscore.com/methodology.shtml](https://www.walkscore.com/methodology.shtml)
- Transit Score: each nearby route gets a "usefulness" value from frequency, route type and distance to nearest stop; values are summed and normalised to 0–100. — [walkscore.com/methodology.shtml](https://www.walkscore.com/methodology.shtml)
- Bike Score: four equally weighted components (hence 25% each, derived): (1) Bike lanes — length of nearby infrastructure with bike paths weighted 2× bike lanes and 3× shared infrastructure, zero beyond 1,000 m, distance decay, from OpenStreetMap; (2) Hills — steepest grade within 200 m radius, 2%–10% grade mapped to 100–0, USGS NED; (3) Destinations and connectivity — a modified Walk Score plus block length and intersection density; (4) Bicycle commuting mode share — 1 km moving window over census tracts, 0%–10% mapped to 0–100 ("safety in numbers"). Bands: 90–100 Biker's Paradise, 70–89 Very Bikeable, 50–69 Bikeable, 0–49 Somewhat Bikeable. Contributors credited: Meghan Winters (SFU), Michael Brauer and Kay Teschke (UBC), CIHR grant. — [walkscore.com/bike-score-methodology.shtml](https://www.walkscore.com/bike-score-methodology.shtml)
- Academic description of Walk Score's weighting: each amenity type has a weight by "level of necessity, e.g., grocery stores are given the highest weight as they are the most frequent walking destination"; for variety categories (restaurants) the p-th nearest instance gets a declining weight; the decay "is originally an exponential function" commonly approximated piecewise-linearly. — [Walkability Optimization: Toronto case study, arXiv 2212.05192](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.05192)
- An open-source replication reports that a fitted specification (decay = 0.008, upper limit 800 m, k = 30 nearest) achieves Spearman ρ = 0.912 against proprietary 2011 Walk Score values. — [Environment and Planning B, DOI 10.1177/23998083251377116](https://doi.org/10.1177/23998083251377116) (not opened directly; the figure came from a search summary)

**15-minute city indices**
- Bruno, Melo, Campanelli & Loreto (Sony CSL Rome), "A universal framework for inclusive 15-minute cities", *Nature Cities*, published September 2024 (Sony CSL gives 18 Sept 2024; Science Media Centre Spain gives 16 Sept 2024). Analyses accessibility to services across "a vast number of cities worldwide" (~10,000) and finds access "highly heterogeneous within and across cities"; proposes a relocation algorithm to tailor solutions per city rather than a standard. — [Sony CSL news](https://sonycsl.co.jp/?p=16605); [Nature Cities article (paywalled/login redirect)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44284-024-00119-4)
- The public tool (version 2.1.0) lets users sort cities by population and by "proximity time" on foot or by bike; data come from OpenStreetMap and were processed with GeoPandas. — [whatif.sonycsl.it/15mincity](https://whatif.sonycsl.it/15mincity/); [Science Media Centre Spain](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/node/4025)
- Expert critique: Francesc Baró (VUB) says the authors "do not take into account the socioeconomic differences of urban populations, that is social equity" — low-density wealthy suburbs lacking services are not comparable to disadvantaged peripheries. Natalie Mueller (ISGlobal): "there is no universal approach for all cities." — [Science Media Centre Spain](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/node/4025)
- Barcelona metropolitan age-sensitive assessment (UPC, 2025) adds an accessibility-quality index across ten service categories and finds only 42% of the elderly population reach all ten within the threshold. — [UPCommons](https://upcommons.upc.edu/entities/publication/e6738652-7f66-44d0-a4f2-a5f80a7238e2)
- A "15min City Score Toolkit – Urban Walkability Analytics" is published on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.14231533). — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/doi/10.5281/zenodo.14231533) (not opened directly)
- Knight Frank's Urban Walkability Tool (London) analysed >140,000 OS AddressBase points against 9,000 amenities to score submarkets — an example of a property-sector walkability index. — [Knight Frank 2022](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2022/9/walkability-quantifying-the-15minute-city)
- A practitioner blog recommends isochrones (reachable-in-15-minutes polygons) rather than straight-line radii for listing-level scores. — [atlas.co](https://atlas.co/blog/map-walkability-scores-near-listings)

**Healthy Streets**
- The Healthy Streets framework has 10 indicators: shade and shelter, easy to cross, welcoming for everyone, places to stop and rest, not too noisy, people feel safe, people choose to walk/cycle, clean air, people feel relaxed, things to see and do. — [Sustrans, Healthy Streets Design Check](https://sustrans.org.uk/for-professionals/urban-design-and-planning/healthy-streets-design-check)
- The 2018 Healthy Streets Check (Lucy Saunders) used 31 metrics mapped onto the 10 indicators to produce a "Healthy Street Check Score"; a later council audit describes the Design Check as 19 metrics each feeding one or more indicators. Sources disagree on metric count; the scoring formula is not public in these documents. — [Saunders 2018 slides](https://londonlivingstreets.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/20180312-lucy-saunders-healthy-streets-check-feb-2018.pdf); [Staffordshire audit example](https://www.staffordshire.gov.uk/Transport/Transport-Planning/Local-transport-plan/Documents/November-2025-update/Appendix-4.1-Example-of-a-Healthy-Streets-Audit.pdf)

**Academic urban liveability index (Higgs et al.)**
- Urban Liveability Index (Melbourne), Higgs et al. 2019, *Int J Health Geographics* 18:14: calculated for 1,550,641 residential address points using a 1,600 m walkable network; destination access uses soft thresholds via a logistic decay function. Seven indicators: walkability (street connectivity, dwelling density, daily-living score), social infrastructure mix, public transport access, large public open space access, affordable housing, local work opportunities (air quality NO₂ only as sensitivity test). — [PMC6558748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558748)
- Aggregation uses the Mazziotta–Pareto Index: outliers >3 SD compressed, each indicator normalised to mean 100 / SD 10 (usual range 70–130), then the address score is the row mean minus a penalty for variability across indicators ("non-compensable" domains). No explicit weights: factor analysis, PCA and Benefit-of-the-Doubt weighting were considered and rejected partly because they are hard to explain to policymakers. The method is described as "transparent and reproducible", scripted in Python with ArcGIS and PostGIS, with destination thresholds taken from Victorian planning standards and an interactive dashboard planned. — [PMC6558748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558748)
- A revised 15-indicator ULI was computed for the same 1.55 M address points using ABS census data. — [RMIT dataset record](https://research-repository.rmit.edu.au/articles/dataset/Urban_Liveability_Index_15_indicators_Melbourne_2012_dataset/13322984)

### Inferences
- "Best practice for transparency" in the literature is procedural rather than a named standard: publish every component indicator and its threshold, use equal or no weights with an explicit formula (ULI, Bike Score), standardise within the study area (z-scores or mean-100/SD-10), and penalise imbalance rather than letting one strong component compensate. A user-reweighting UI is consistent with this but I found no peer-reviewed source prescribing it (see Gaps).
- Walk Score's proprietary weights mean a project cannot legally or practically replicate "Walk Score" by name; an open replication at ρ ≈ 0.91 shows that an exponential-decay amenity score over OSM/Overture POIs is a sufficient substitute.
- Including an activity-intensity layer in a liveability composite has no precedent among the indices reviewed; Bike Score's "bicycle commuting mode share" (census-derived, 0–10% → 0–100) is the closest analogue and suggests presenting Strava heat as a capped, normalised 0–100 sub-score rather than raw counts.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Nature Cities methods section, so the exact service categories, population grid (hexagonal or otherwise), walking speed and inequality metric used by the Sony CSL tool remain unconfirmed.
- No source found that specifies the Healthy Streets Design Check scoring arithmetic.
- No peer-reviewed source found that evaluates user-adjustable weights in liveability dashboards; the recommendation to "let users reweight" is inference from the ULI authors' stated motivation (explainability to policymakers).
- Nieuwenhuijsen's own 15-minute-city index methodology did not surface; the ISGlobal link found is Mueller's commentary on the Sony CSL paper, not a separate index.

## 3. Front-end and tiles: recommended 2025–2026 stack for serving vector/raster tiles cheaply, and indicative monthly cost for ~10k monthly users

### Takeaway
The current low-cost standard is: tippecanoe → PMTiles archive → Cloudflare R2 (no egress fees, 10 GB and 10 M reads free per month) → MapLibre GL JS with the pmtiles protocol, optionally deck.gl's H3HexagonLayer for hex overlays; Protomaps' own calculator puts a site with 625,000 sessions / 10 M tile requests per month at about $11.45 on Cloudflare versus $119.56 on AWS, and real hobby deployments report $0–$2 per month.

### Cited Findings

**Formats and libraries**
- PMTiles is "pyramids of map tiles in a single file on static storage"; spec version 3; reference implementations BSD-3-Clause, spec public domain/CC0; clients: MapLibre GL JS (≥ v1.15 for demos), Leaflet, OpenLayers; tooling: `pmtiles convert` (MBTiles→PMTiles), `pmtiles upload`, and `tippecanoe -o out.pmtiles` writes PMTiles directly. GitHub ≈ 3.1k stars. — [github.com/protomaps/PMTiles](https://github.com/protomaps/PMTiles)
- Cloud storage guidance: "R2 is the recommended storage platform for PMTiles" (no bandwidth fees); each HTTP Range request counts as a GET; CORS must allow GET/HEAD, headers `range` and `if-match`, and expose `etag`; S3, Azure, DigitalOcean Spaces, Backblaze B2 and Scaleway are HTTP/1.1-only; GitHub Pages works up to 1 GB per repo. — [docs.protomaps.com/pmtiles/cloud-storage](https://docs.protomaps.com/pmtiles/cloud-storage)
- Martin (maplibre/martin): Rust tile server for PostGIS tables and functions, PMTiles (local or HTTP), MBTiles, GeoJSON, and GeoParquet via DuckDB (marked unstable); composite sources, sprites, fonts, `martin cp` bulk generation; ≈ 4.0k stars; Apache-2.0 OR MIT. — [github.com/maplibre/martin](https://github.com/maplibre/martin)
- deck.gl `H3HexagonLayer` renders hexagons from H3 indices, coloured/extruded by a per-cell value; `h3-js` must load before deck.gl when using pre-bundled scripts. — [deck.gl docs](https://deck.gl/docs/api-reference/geo-layers/h3-hexagon-layer)
- Lonboard (Python) exposes `before_id` to slot a deck.gl layer into the MapLibre basemap stack, effective only with a MapLibre basemap in "interleaved" mode. — [developmentseed.org/lonboard H3HexagonLayer](https://developmentseed.org/lonboard/latest/api/layers/h3-hexagon-layer/)
- MapLibre's Flutter binding also documents PMTiles use. — [maplibre.org/flutter-maplibre-gl](https://maplibre.org/flutter-maplibre-gl/advanced/pmtiles/)

**Costs**
- Cloudflare R2 pricing (fetched October 2026): Standard storage $0.015/GB-month; Class A operations $4.50 per million; Class B (reads) $0.36 per million; egress free via Workers, S3 API or r2.dev; free tier per month: 10 GB-month storage, 1 M Class A, 10 M Class B; unauthorized (401) requests not charged. — [developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)
- Protomaps cost calculator worked example (625,000 sessions, 10 M tile GETs, 1,000 GB egress, 50% cache hit, 110 GB stored): Cloudflare total $11.45/month (Workers paid plan $5.00 + 10 M invocations $3.00 + 5 M R2 GETs $1.80 + 110 GB storage $1.65 + $0 bandwidth) versus AWS CloudFront+Lambda+S3 $119.56/month (of which $100 is bandwidth); Google Maps Dynamic Maps SKU for the same sessions $3,640/month; a hosted map API $1,875/month. Implied ≈16 tile requests per session. — [docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cost](https://docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cost)
- Protomaps Cloudflare integration: Workers plan $5/month including 10 M requests, then $0.50 per additional million; R2 storage/reads billed only on cache misses. — [docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cloudflare](https://docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cloudflare) (not opened directly; figure from search summary)
- Pinball Map's self-hosted Protomaps tiles on Cloudflare cost $1.67 in the first month with the next month expected near $0. — [blog.pinballmap.com, Nov 2024](https://blog.pinballmap.com/2024/11/05/protomaps-tile-hosting/)
- FOSS4G 2023 talk: a ~90 GB planet OSM PMTiles on R2 serving 35 M tile requests cost about $1.35/month at then-current pricing. — [talks.osgeo.org FOSS4G 2023](https://talks.osgeo.org/foss4g-2023/talk/UYFZ9B/) (not opened directly)
- Protomaps blog estimate: 50,000 Google Maps loads ≈ $350/month vs ≈ $0.50 of Workers request fees on Cloudflare (vendor estimate). — [protomaps.com/blog/serverless-maps-now-open-source](https://protomaps.com/blog/serverless-maps-now-open-source)

### Inferences
- For a hobby site with ~10k monthly users: at Protomaps' observed ≈16 tile requests per session, 10k sessions ≈ 160k Class B reads; even at a pessimistic 100 requests per session (1 M reads) the site stays inside R2's 10 M free reads and 10 GB free storage if the city-scale PMTiles (basemap extract + hex/score layer) are under 10 GB. Expected recurring cost is therefore $0–$5/month (the $5 only if a Worker is added for caching/custom domain logic), plus domain. A PostGIS/Martin server is unnecessary at this scale and would add $5–$20/month of VPS cost.
- Serving the hex scores as a static PMTiles vector layer (one feature per H3 cell with score attributes, styled with MapLibre data-driven expressions) avoids the need for deck.gl unless 3D extrusion or client-side reweighting across hundreds of thousands of cells is required; deck.gl H3HexagonLayer is the better fit when users reweight components live, because the per-cell colour is recomputed in JavaScript from attribute columns without regenerating tiles.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 benchmark comparing Martin vs pg_tileserv vs static PMTiles under load was found.
- I did not verify current MapLibre GL JS or tippecanoe version numbers/stars (GitHub API access was blocked in this session; only pages fetched via WebFetch yielded star counts).

## 4. Back-end and data pipeline: DuckDB / GeoParquet / PostGIS / H3 / osmnx pipeline shapes for GPS traces or counter data → hex scores

### Takeaway
The documented 2024–2026 pattern is a batch pipeline in DuckDB (spatial + h3 community extensions) reading GeoParquet (including Overture Maps data directly from object storage via httpfs), assigning each point/segment to an H3 cell, `GROUP BY` cell with a `HAVING count >= k` suppression step, then writing GeoParquet/GeoJSON for tippecanoe; PostGIS remains the choice when per-address network analysis (ULI-style 1,600 m walkable catchments) is needed.

### Cited Findings
- DuckDB H3 extension: `INSTALL h3 FROM community; LOAD h3;` — implements hierarchical hexagonal indexing; the documented example converts lat/lng to H3 at resolution 9, computes cell boundaries as WKT, counts points per cell and filters low-count cells with `HAVING`. — [DuckDB community extensions announcement](https://duckdb.org/2024/07/05/community-extensions); extension list [duckdb.org/docs/extensions/community_extensions](https://duckdb.org/docs/extensions/community_extensions)
- DuckDB spatial extension provides the GEOMETRY type and GeoParquet read/write. — [duckdb.org/docs/extensions/spatial](https://duckdb.org/docs/extensions/spatial)
- "With its spatial extension and httpfs module, DuckDB can query GeoParquet files directly from cloud storage using familiar SQL syntax", demonstrated with Overture Maps data. — [cng-python.gishub.org DuckDB geospatial chapter](https://cng-python.gishub.org/book/analytics/duckdb-geospatial)
- FOSS4G NA 2025 talk: because DuckDB has no native raster support, raster layers are re-aggregated to H3 hexagons inside DuckDB; "hexagonal grids offer advantages over traditional square pixels for analytical purposes." — [talks.osgeo.org FOSS4G-NA 2025](https://talks.osgeo.org/foss4g-na-2025/talk/FUYA37/)
- R users can use `duckh3`, which wraps the DuckDB H3 extension lazily with dplyr/sf. — [MyNixOS package record](https://mynixos.com/nixpkgs/package/rPackages.duckh3)
- Martin can convert GeoParquet to vector tiles on the fly via DuckDB (unstable) and bulk-generate MBTiles with `martin cp`. — [github.com/maplibre/martin](https://github.com/maplibre/martin)
- The Urban Liveability Index pipeline (address-point network catchments, logistic decay to destinations) was scripted in Python with ArcGIS and PostGIS — the reference pattern for network-based rather than hex-based scoring. — [PMC6558748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558748)
- Local heatmap generators ingest Strava bulk-export archives containing `.fit`, `.gpx`, `.tcx` (some gzipped) plus `activities.csv`; the roboes tool unzips, repairs `.tcx`, filters by bounding box and reverse-geocodes via Nominatim. — [github.com/roboes/strava-local-heatmap-tool](https://github.com/roboes/strava-local-heatmap-tool)
- hotpot (erik) is "a renderer and tile server that renders customizable activity heatmap images from GPS tracks extracted from GPX, TCX, and FIT files", able to ingest Strava export CSV metadata, designed to run locally or self-hosted. — [github.com/erik/hotpot](https://github.com/erik/hotpot) (star count not retrieved)
- Bias correction code (LASSO + Poisson regression linking Strava to official counts with census covariates) is published. — [github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction](https://github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction)
- Strava Metro delivers edge-level counts on the street network per time window (see §1); Strava's Global Heatmap is updated monthly and shows the last year of activity. — [Strava support: Global Heatmap](https://support.strava.com/hc/en-us/articles/216918877-Global-Heatmap)

### Inferences
- Typical pipeline shape, assembled from the sources above: (1) ingest Strava Metro edge CSV/shapefile (or counter CSVs / GPX exports) → GeoParquet; (2) in DuckDB, join to an OSM/Overture street network and either keep segment granularity or `h3_latlng_to_cell` at resolution 8–9 (res 9 ≈ 0.1 km² cells, as used in the DuckDB example); (3) compute per-cell counts, apply a `HAVING unique_contributors >= 3`-style suppression, and fit a bias-correction regression against any available counters plus census covariates (Roy et al. pattern); (4) compute other components (amenity decay scores from Overture/OSM POIs with osmnx or DuckDB spatial, transit, green space) per cell; (5) standardise each to mean-100/SD-10 or percentile, aggregate with ULI-style mean-minus-imbalance or equal weights; (6) write GeoJSON/FlatGeobuf → `tippecanoe -o scores.pmtiles` → R2.
- Because hex counts from monthly heat are stable but daily ones are not (§1), a monthly or annual batch job is sufficient; there is no need for a live database or API for 10k monthly users.

### Gaps
- No single published end-to-end GeoParquet→H3→PMTiles pipeline with a stated resolution strategy was found; the shape above is a synthesis.
- osmnx-specific documentation was not fetched in this session; its role (street-network download and isochrone/catchment computation) is stated from general knowledge and should be verified by the writer if quoted.

## 6. Privacy and legal design: GDPR for aggregated activity near homes, home-location inference, minimum-count thresholds, k-anonymity, heatmap privacy zones

### Takeaway
The EDPB treats location traces as "notoriously difficult to anonymise", requires anonymisation to be judged on the dataset as a whole against singling-out, linkability and inference, says poorly aggregated location data is not anonymous, and "highly encourage[s]" transparency about the anonymisation method; a 2023 NC State study showed Strava's aggregated heatmap still revealed home addresses of active users in sparse areas (17 of 20 manually checked matches showed a clear heat "notch"; 31.7–37.5% discovery likelihood), which is the concrete risk a neighbourhood map must design against with contributor-count thresholds, suppression near residential buildings, and coarsened resolution.

### Cited Findings
- EDPB Guidelines 04/2020 (21 April 2020), §2.2: ¶15 anonymisation must defeat linkage "against any 'reasonable' effort", accounting for "rarity of a phenomenon including population density"; ¶16 robustness is judged on "(i) singling-out ... (ii) linkability ... (iii) inference"; ¶18 "Data cannot be anonymised on their own, meaning that only datasets as a whole may or may not be made anonymous"; ¶19 location data are "known to be notoriously difficult to anonymise"; ¶20 "Mobility traces of individuals are inherently highly correlated and unique"; ¶21 a single individual's trace over time "cannot be fully anonymised ... This also holds for location data that is poorly aggregated"; ¶22 anonymisation requires "processing data from a reasonably large set of individuals"; ¶23 "transparency regarding the anonymisation methodology is highly encouraged." Footnotes cite de Montjoye et al. 2013 "Unique in the Crowd" and Pyrgelis et al. 2017 on membership inference on aggregate location data. — [EDPB Guidelines 04/2020 PDF](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_20200420_contact_tracing_covid_with_annex_en.pdf)
- ICO position (as reported by a law firm): "generalised location data trend analysis based on properly anonymised and aggregated mobile phone data" falls outside the UK GDPR and DPA 2018. — [CMS Law, 2020](https://cms.law/en/bgr/legal-updates/location-apps-in-the-time-of-covid-19) (secondary)
- Childs, Nolting & Das (NC State), "Heat Marks the Spot: De-Anonymizing Users' Geographical Data on the Strava Heatmap", IEEE ConPro '23: crawled the public heatmap at zoom 17.33 across whole US states, detected heat "notches" entering residential parcels, matched them to users found via Strava's city search and to voter-registration records. Of 20 manually examined matches, 17 showed a clear notch (false positives: a trail, a neighbour, a tiny heat artefact); within a 100 m threshold the chance of discovering a user was 31.7%, rising to 37.5% for a user with the dataset-average 308 activities; "in areas with only a few active Strava users, the heat generated by one individual can be clearly visible", while dense areas are hard to exploit; with 20 site visits an attacker could target 262 of 638 cities. Proposed mitigations: "Targeted Heat Removal" (do not store heat concentrated near house footprints from OSM) and rounding/snapping the heat trace to the nearest street. The paper also cites prior work breaking 85% of Strava endpoint privacy zones using the gap between reported and mapped distance. — [ConPro '23 PDF](https://conpro23.ieee-security.org/papers/childs-conpro23.pdf); press coverage [BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/strava-heatmap-feature-can-be-abused-to-find-home-addresses/), [Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2023/06/strava-heatmap-loophole-may-reveal-users-home-addresses)
- The ConPro paper quotes Strava that "data within hidden zones of activities that are shared with 'Followers' or 'Everyone' will now be used in de-identified aggregated data", i.e. per-activity privacy zones do not remove data from the aggregate heatmap. — [ConPro '23 PDF](https://conpro23.ieee-security.org/papers/childs-conpro23.pdf)
- Strava Global Heatmap rules: includes only activities set to "Everyone"; excludes portions hidden by map-visibility settings (e.g. hidden start/end) and users who opted out at strava.com/settings/privacy; "heat isn't shown for an area until multiple users have uploaded activities through it in the last year" (no number given); updated monthly; shows the last year of activity. — [Strava support: Global Heatmap](https://support.strava.com/hc/en-us/articles/216918877-Global-Heatmap). (The ConPro paper states the heatmap uses the last two years; Strava's current page says one year — sources conflict, probably reflecting a change over time.)
- Strava Metro excludes private activities and deleted accounts and describes its data as "aggregated and de-identified"; the ≥3-unique-users-per-segment rule and rounding to multiples of 5 are reported in academic literature (see §1) but not on the Strava pages reached. — [Strava Metro help](https://stravametro.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360045856414-How-Strava-activities-contribute-to-Strava-Metro-data)
- Raturi et al. 2021 quantify the utility cost of Strava's binning (significant information loss, possible reversal of conclusions). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jotrge/v92y2021ics0966692321000739.html)
- The 2018 Strava heatmap incident (military bases revealed) was addressed by governments restricting app use, not by Strava. — [ConPro '23 PDF](https://conpro23.ieee-security.org/papers/childs-conpro23.pdf)
- Strava's API agreement additionally imposes contractual limits (display only to the authenticating athlete; delete on termination; "appropriate security measures"). — [strava.com/legal/api](https://www.strava.com/legal/api)

### Inferences
- A neighbourhood attractiveness map that renders activity intensity at building-visible zoom levels inherits the ConPro attack surface. Design controls that follow directly from the sources: (1) never publish below a minimum number of distinct contributors per cell/segment (Strava's ≥3 is the industry floor; the EDPB's "reasonably large set of individuals" implies higher where population density is low); (2) cap the maximum zoom or coarsen H3 resolution in sparse areas (risk scales inversely with density per ConPro and EDPB ¶15); (3) drop heat within a buffer of residential building footprints or snap to street centrelines (ConPro mitigations); (4) publish the anonymisation method (EDPB ¶23); (5) treat the aggregated layer, not individual traces, as the only stored artefact if the site is to claim it holds no personal data.
- If the site ingests individual GPX exports contributed by users (dérive model) it is a GDPR controller for those raw traces and needs a lawful basis, retention policy and deletion path; processing in the browser only (as dérive does) avoids this.

### Gaps
- I found no EDPB or ICO document that names a specific k (minimum contributors) for mobility aggregates; "k-anonymity" as a term did not appear in the regulator texts reached.
- I could not confirm whether Strava has, since 2023, added heatmap-specific privacy zones beyond the existing map-visibility settings.
- No DPA enforcement decision concerning an aggregated activity heatmap was found.

## 7. Business and sustainability: how indie geo sites survive, and what real-estate portals did with walkability/activity layers

### Takeaway
Indie activity-map sites survive on low-priced annual subscriptions (VeloViewer PRO £10/yr, PRO+ £20/yr; CityStrides "Supporter"; Wandrer paid full history), while the real-estate precedent is Zillow's 2009 adoption of Walk Score (later Transit Score) on listings; I found no evidence that Rightmove, Zoopla or Idealista display walkability or activity layers, and no documented case of an activity-heat layer being licensed to a portal.

### Cited Findings
- Walk Score was adopted in January 2009 by real-estate sites with >80 million listings including Zillow.com, ZipRealty.com and Postlets.com, computed automatically per property. — [Inman News, 2009](https://www.inman.com/2009/01/08/listings-get-walkability-ratings/)
- Zillow later expanded the partnership to include Transit Score. — [Zillow Group newsroom](https://www.zillowgroup.com/news/your-homes-transit-score-now-available-on-zillow-3)
- Zillow moved from community-level to address-level Walk Scores around 2011 (adjacent addresses could differ, e.g. 51 vs 60). — [LinkNKY, 2011](https://linknky.com/uncategorized/2011/04/20/fort-thomas-walking-score-update/)
- Walk Score covers any address in the US, UK, Canada and Australia. — [Wikipedia: Walk Score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walk_Score)
- MLS systems (e.g. SmartMLS) embed Walk Score in listing reports. — [SmartMLS help](https://connectmls.smartmls.com/hc/en-us/articles/23015297642139-Walk-Score)
- Knight Frank (UK agency) built its own walkability tool for research/marketing rather than a portal feature. — [Knight Frank 2022](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2022/9/walkability-quantifying-the-15minute-city)
- Mapbox Movement is still a live, licensed product ("global privacy-forward dataset of de-identified and aggregated mobile device activity"; access restricted to customers who purchased a licence; sample data for evaluation) — a potential B2B data cost rather than a revenue model. — [docs.mapbox.com/data/movement/overview](https://docs.mapbox.com/data/movement/overview/)
- Strava Metro has been free for qualifying urban planners and advocacy organisations since 2020 (not for commercial sites). — [The Register, Sept 2020](https://www.theregister.com/2020/09/28/strava_free_data/); [GlobeNewswire Nov 2020](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2020/11/24/2132700/0/en/Strava-Debuted-a-Metro-Service-to-Provide-Anonymous-Data-for-Urban-Planners-and-Transport-Managers.html)
- VeloViewer pricing: free tier limited; PRO £10/year, PRO+ £20/year. — [blog.veloviewer.com](https://blog.veloviewer.com/?p=2237)
- CityStrides monetises via a Supporter tier (filtering, priority processing). — [community.citystrides.com](https://community.citystrides.com/t/have-a-city-pedal-bike-site/14336?page=2)
- Wandrer charges for uploading full ride history (user report). — [forums.electricbikereview.com](https://forums.electricbikereview.com/threads/2025-our-rides-in-words-photos-maps-and-videos.57230/post-702343)
- Strava's June 2026 program adds a developer cost (Strava subscription, ~US$12/month per heise) and bans intermediary platforms, raising the floor cost for any indie app relying on the API. — [Strava Community Hub](https://communityhub.strava.com/insider-journal-9/an-update-to-our-developer-program-13428); [heise](https://heise.de/-11315017)
- Protomaps/Cloudflare infrastructure costs for a 625k-session/month site are ≈ $11.45/month, so hosting is not the binding cost for a hobby project. — [docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cost](https://docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cost)

### Inferences
- With infrastructure at $0–$12/month, sustainability is dominated by data access (Strava Metro is free only to planners/advocates; Mapbox Movement is a paid licence) and developer time, not hosting. The observed indie pattern (annual £10–£20 supporter tiers, features gated behind payment) is the only monetisation model with documented uptake among comparable sites; affiliate deals with portals or a B2B planner API were not evidenced for any comparable indie site.
- The Zillow/Walk Score history shows portals adopt a third-party neighbourhood score when it is address-level, nationally complete, and explainable in one sentence; an activity-heat score with sparse coverage and demographic bias caveats (§1) would struggle to meet the first two criteria.

### Gaps
- No information found on Rightmove, Zoopla or Idealista walkability/activity features despite two targeted searches; absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
- No Statshunters business information found.
- No source found quantifying revenue or user numbers for VeloViewer, CityStrides or Wandrer.
- No documented example of a real-estate portal licensing Strava-derived or other activity-heat data.
