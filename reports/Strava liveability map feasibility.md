# Build the Liveability Map Without Strava

A website that scores neighbourhoods for how good they are to run, walk and live in is feasible and cheap to operate, but it cannot be built on Strava data, and no other fitness tracker fills the gap. As of October 2026 every route to area-level Strava data is closed by contract: the API Policy (effective 1 June 2026) forbids displaying other users' data, aggregating it, storing geographic information or caching anything beyond seven days; the Terms of Service (28 September 2026) ban automated collection of heatmap tiles "regardless of whether you are logged into a Strava account"; and Strava Metro's free licence excludes real-estate uses, forbids public distribution of the data and requires 30-day pre-publication review of any output. The viral thesis is also weaker than its framing: Jacob Swiney's own Indianapolis regression explains **only 25% of tract-level income variance (R² = 0.25)**, and a decade of prior analyses plus the academic literature show that Strava heat measures who records activity (disproportionately affluent, male, 35–54-year-old cyclists) rather than where life is good. The recommended path is an open-data "active-living index" built from OpenStreetMap infrastructure, census grids, Copernicus and EEA environmental layers and municipal counter feeds, served as static PMTiles from Cloudflare R2 for **roughly $0–12 per month in hosting**, with a consented, browser-processed user-upload layer added later if the site earns an audience. That product is legally clean (CC BY and ODbL attribution), covers Europe wall-to-wall, and answers the question people are actually asking of the heatmap without inheriting Strava's platform risk, demographic bias or home-address privacy exposure. The verdict, in one line: build the map, but build it on supply-side open data and treat Strava as an inspiration, not an input.

## The viral "wealth map" thesis explains a quarter of income variance and is a decade old

The LinkedIn post by Cristiano Galvão is derivative commentary rather than original analysis: it re-shares Jacob Swiney's 28 August 2026 Substack essay, frames Strava running heatmaps as continuously updated "wealth maps" that lead official data by two to five years, and had 14 reactions at the time of research ([LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cristianogalvao_people-are-using-strava-to-find-the-best-activity-7500762538289328128-QeY2)). Swiney's essay is the substantive source. His method reads the blue trail pixels from Global Heatmap screenshots, georeferences the image to town labels to within about 290 metres, scores roughly 8,000 grid cells of about 320 metres across Marion County, Indiana, and joins them to 227 census tracts with 2023 ACS income, rent and population. Regressing log income on log running intensity across 225 tracts yields **R² = 0.25, t = 8.6 and about 13% higher median income per doubling of running intensity**; a "gap" map (running z-score minus income z-score) then shortlists tracts where running exceeds income as possible early-improvement signals ([Substack](https://cjacobswiney.substack.com/p/using-strava-to-underwrite-apartment)). Swiney is candid about the limits: busy trails such as the Monon inflate intensity without any demographic change, the census lags, he "can't tell which story is which from just looking at the map", the mismatch map is a shortlist rather than a conclusion, and no code repository is published ([Substack](https://cjacobswiney.substack.com/p/using-strava-to-underwrite-apartment)). The post's "2–5 year lead" claim is stronger than Swiney's own "2-year lag" and is unsourced.

The surrounding trend is a social-media rediscovery of an old observation. The 2026 wave began on 24 August when @NYCerInExile called the Chicago heatmap "a giant proxy for class and wealth" (5.3 million views), was amplified the next day by @Terravoir's "use Strava as a proxy for where to live" (1.9 million views), and within hours replies had reframed wealth as safety, with one account overlaying NYPD precinct crime data ([X post](https://x.com/NYCerInExile/status/2092008651288351088); [X trending summary](https://x.com/i/trending/2092631905849983370)). Pieter Levels, creator of Hoodmaps, responded on 27 August that "Strava's data is quite protected so I couldn't index it for Hoodmaps" and added a US Census income mode instead, a telling signal that the most capable indie map-maker in the space found the door shut ([X post](https://x.com/levelsio/status/2092907465629135100)). Analysts had drawn the identical heatmap-equals-wealth conclusion in Kansas City in 2014 ([Equitable Growth](https://equitablegrowth.org/astonishing-imprint-pattern-racial-housing-segregation-jogging-routes-kansas-city/)), in Florida in 2015 ([Policy blog](https://policypolicy.wordpress.com/2015/05/13/strava-maps-urban-wealth-and-inequality/)), and in Baltimore and New York in Alison Désir's 2022 *Running While Black*, where redlined Harlem and Brooklyn were "nearly dark" ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/strava-heatmap-running-while-black-1849677409)). Mainstream outlets (NYT, Guardian, CityLab, Wired) do not appear to have covered the 2026 round; the coverage came from ZME Science, Domus and campus press.

What is genuinely new in 2026 is the explicit "pick where to live or invest" framing and the first quantitative follow-up, and both attracted immediate, well-founded pushback. Strava itself, quoted by Domus, says the heatmap "cannot be used to compare how many people are active in one place versus another" because the visualisation is normalised and not everyone uses the app ([Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2026/09/02/strava-where-to-live-better-city-maps.html)). The Daily Trojan's October 2026 analysis of Los Angeles argues the map shows where running is *easy* (coast, Griffith Park) rather than where people want to run, that a dark patch "does not mean residents don't run there", and that Strava "cannot distinguish between heat, dangerous crossings, and simply fewer app users" ([Daily Trojan](https://dailytrojan.com/2026/10/16/stravas-heatmap-shows-us-where-running-in-la-is-possible/)). The peer-reviewed record is thin and indirect: a 2023 Boston study found street environments perceived as accessible, open, clean, safe and natural correlate with heatmap running ([HKU record](https://repository.hku.hk/handle/10722/336372)), and a 2022 Chengdu study found heatmap-derived cycling and running positively correlated with housing prices ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9101655/)). No peer-reviewed study tests heatmap intensity as a predictor of liveability, house-price change or gentrification in the US or Europe; the Domus-cited Mecklenburg County renovation-permit paper could not be located and should be treated as unverified. The honest reading is that the heatmap is a real but weak and heavily confounded correlate of affluence, and that what it visibly traces (parks, waterfronts, trails, continuous sidewalks) is the kind of thing open infrastructure data already describes.

## Every Strava route to area-level data is contractually closed in 2026

Strava has spent 2024–2026 closing, one by one, each way a third party could obtain "where people run" data, and the closures are legal as well as technical. The API never had a heatmap or aggregate endpoint; the only discovery endpoint, Explore Segments, was restricted to approved Extended Access apps on 1 September 2026, and the Club endpoints were removed the same day ([Strava V3 API Changelog](https://developers.strava.com/docs/changelog/)). New apps start in "single-player mode" limited to the developer's own account, a Strava subscription is required to create one, self-service raises capacity to 10 athletes, and anything beyond needs a case-by-case review that Strava says is "not a guarantee" ([Getting Started](https://developers.strava.com/docs/getting-started/); [Rate Limits](https://developers.strava.com/docs/rate-limits/)). Default read limits are **100 requests per 15 minutes and 1,000 per day** ([Rate Limits](https://developers.strava.com/docs/rate-limits/)), and the subscription costs about **US$11.99 per month or $79.99 per year** according to press reports, with Strava citing a 448% year-on-year rise in developer applications and aggressive AI scraping as the reason ([heise.de](https://heise.de/-11315017); [Strava Community Hub](https://communityhub.strava.com/insider-journal-9/an-update-to-our-developer-program-13428)).

Even if the rate limits were no obstacle, the API Policy effective 1 June 2026 prohibits every element of a public map in express terms. Section 2.3 states that Strava data "provided by a specific Strava user may be displayed or disclosed in your Developer Application only to that user", and that you "may not display or disclose Strava Data related to other users, even if such data is publicly viewable on the Strava Platform" ([API Policy](https://www.strava.com/legal/api_policy)). Section 5.4 bans processing Strava data "even publicly viewable Strava Data, including in an aggregated, de-identified, or anonymized manner, for the purposes of analytics"; section 5.5 bans accumulating data "through repeated authorized API calls into a corpus, dataset, archive, or database" and storing it in any "Persistent Index"; section 5.7 bans aggregating, caching or storing "geographic location information"; section 6.2 caps any cache at **seven days**; section 5.3 bans any AI/ML use including "ingestion into a context window"; and section 5.10 forbids transfer to third parties "even if a user of your Developer Application consents" ([API Policy](https://www.strava.com/legal/api_policy)). Strava's own November 2024 announcement named the target use explicitly: the display rule exists to stop data being shown to others "for example, in a public feed or heatmap" ([Strava Press](https://press.strava.com/articles/updates-to-stravas-api-agreement)). There is no consent-based carve-out, and the Agreement reminds developers that API access "is a privilege granted to you by Strava, not a right" ([API Agreement](https://www.strava.com/legal/api)).

The Global Heatmap tiles, the route Swiney used, are technically reachable and contractually off-limits. Street-level detail has required a login since March 2018, after the heatmap exposed military bases ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018/03/13/after-exposing-secret-military-bases-strava-restricts-data-visi/)). High-zoom tiles are served through signed CloudFront cookies that expire weekly, Strava deployed new hardened URLs in late March 2025 that broke direct TMS access, and the OSM community's JOSM extension was non-functional from May to September 2026 ([OSM Wiki: Strava](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Strava)). The Terms of Service effective 28 September 2026 then closed the legal door: "Automated access to or collection of data from the Services—by any means, including data mining, robots, screen scraping, scripts, or similar data-gathering tools or software such as browser extensions and crawlers—is prohibited. This prohibition applies regardless of whether you are logged into a Strava account", and the same document claims "any aggregated data based on Content on the Services" as Strava's proprietary information and bans derivative works ([Strava Terms of Service](https://www.strava.com/legal/terms)). The only known permission is an informal grant to OpenStreetMap mappers to trace into OSM only; the wiki directs "other non-personal uses" to Strava Metro, and OSM's own licensing veterans call the permission "hand wavy" and revocable ([OSM Wiki: Strava](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Strava); [OSM Community Forum](https://community.openstreetmap.org/t/strava-mapping-legal-usage/7104)). A one-off blog post built on manual screenshots sits in a grey zone; a website that recaptures tiles quarterly, as Swiney plans to, is automated collection producing a derivative aggregate, which is exactly what the ToS forbids.

Strava Metro, the legitimate bulk product, is free but not for this. It is available only to organisations that "plan, own, or maintain active transportation or outdoor recreation infrastructure" or seek to influence such planning, plus vetted consultants and accredited academics, and the FAQ says flatly that "Strava doesn't partner with real estate investors, financial services companies, or retailers" ([Strava Metro Terms](https://metro.strava.com/terms); [Strava Metro FAQ](https://metro.strava.com/faq)). The Metro Terms (last updated 4 September 2026) confine use to a single approved project, forbid public distribution of the underlying data including Edge Counts, require every external report to be submitted to Strava **thirty days before publication** for approval, ban AI/ML use and "competitive products", and limit screenshots to advocacy and grant contexts ([Strava Metro Terms](https://metro.strava.com/terms)). A continuously updated public website is not a "Licensee Report"; even a city that holds Metro data cannot republish it, as NYSDOT and West Northamptonshire Council state explicitly ([511NY Rideshare](https://511nyrideshare.org/web/analytics-sandbox-for-emerging-mobility-data-sources/strava); [Northampton Pure](https://pure.northampton.ac.uk/en/datasets/strava-metro-data-for-the-baseline-phase-march-2022-of-the-dft-ac/)).

Enforcement history shows Strava does not need to sue: it revokes tokens. The November 2024 display rule forced VeloViewer to drop its heatmap tiles and other users' efforts, Intervals.icu to hide Strava activities from coaches and followers, and CityStrides (a public street-completion map, the closest analogue to the proposed product) to hide Strava-sourced activities from all public pages within weeks ([DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/11/stravas-changes-to-kill-off-apps.html); [CityStrides community thread](https://community.citystrides.com/t/new-strava-api-terms-dont-allow-sharing-activity-data-to-other-people/28433)). Wandrer publicly disagreed that showing a user's map to other users violated the terms and still had to comply ([news.wandrer.earth](https://news.wandrer.earth/2024/12/09/strava-wandrer-changes.html)). The one lawsuit on record, Strava v. Garmin (filed 30 September 2025), asserted Strava's patents on Segments and heatmap-based "preference map" routing, which signals that Strava regards heatmaps as defended IP; it was voluntarily dismissed without prejudice after 21 days ([Marathon Handbook](https://marathonhandbook.com/strava-sues-garmin/); [Bicycle Retailer](https://bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2025/10/22/strava-drops-suit-against-garmin)). Every tightening since 2018 followed a misuse incident, and Strava filed confidentially for an IPO in February 2026 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/01/strava-declares-war-on-scrapers-ahead-of-ipo)); a product that visibly repurposes heat as a wealth or safety map is precisely the kind of use that provokes the next restriction.

| Route to Strava "where people run" data | Technically obtainable? | Permitted for a public map? | Governing text |
|---|---|---|---|

The last row is the only Strava-adjacent route left open, and it is a cold-start crowdsourcing model rather than a data source: the site would have to recruit its own contributors and would hold nothing until it did.

## Other trackers offer nothing, but open infrastructure and census data cover Europe for free

No fitness platform other than Strava exposes a redistributable aggregate, and the ecosystem is tightening rather than opening. Garmin's Trendline popularity heatmap exists only inside Connect and Edge devices with no API, and Garmin paused all new applications to its Connect Developer Program in spring 2026 citing a "significant redesign" with no resumption date ([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/09/14/garmin-developer-api-access-paused/); [Garmin forum FAQ](https://forums.garmin.com/apps-software/mobile-apps-web/f/garmin-connect-web/162520/faq---segments-heatmap)). Komoot's API "is only available to partners, who signed a corresponding partner contract" ([komoot API docs](https://static.komoot.de/doc/external-api/v007/index.html)); Suunto states "We currently don't offer the API access for personal use" ([Suunto API Zone](https://apizone.suunto.com/)); Polar and Wahoo offer per-user OAuth access to an individual's own workouts and nothing aggregate ([Polar blog](https://www.polar.com/blog/introducing-polar-open-accesslink-api/); [Wahoo developers](https://developers.wahooligan.com/cloud)); and the legacy Fitbit Web API shuts off on 30 October 2026 with Google Fit's REST API following by year end ([Google Health API](https://developers.google.com/health/about); [Sahha blog](https://sahha.ai/blog/google-fit-api-sunset-migration/)). Commercial mobility vendors are sales-only: Mapbox Movement is "restricted to Mapbox customers who have purchased a Mapbox Movement license" ([Mapbox Movement overview](https://docs.mapbox.com/data/movement/overview/)), Replica prices by jurisdiction population for public agencies ([Replica pricing](https://www.replicahq.com/pricing)), and StreetLight publishes no prices. Open GPS corpora are either tiny and mapper-biased (OpenStreetMap's `/api/0.6/trackpoints`, ODbL, 0.25° bounding boxes and 5,000 points per page) or academic-only (UCSD's FitRec/Endomondo set of 253,020 workouts is "for academic use only") ([OSM wiki API v0.6](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/API_v0.6); [FitRec datasets](https://mcauleylab.ucsd.edu/public_datasets/gdrive/fitrec/FitRec-Project.html)).

The legally clean "real activity" signal is fixed counters, which are excellent for calibration and useless for coverage. Paris publishes hourly bicycle counts per permanent counter on a rolling 13 months through an Opendatasoft API ([Paris Data](https://opendata.paris.fr/explore/dataset/comptage-velo-donnees-compteurs/information/)); New York's historical Eco-Counter dataset holds **7,378,266 rows from 2012 to January 2026** via the SODA API ([NYC Open Data](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/api/views/uczf-rk3c.json)); Dublin's pedestrian and cycle counter API updates every 15 minutes under CC BY 4.0 ([data.gov.ie](https://data.gov.ie/en_GB/dataset/pedestrian-and-cycle-counter-api-for-dublin-region)); Berlin publishes 20 permanent stations daily ([Berlin VIZ](https://viz.berlin.de/en/traffic-in-berlin/bike-counters/)). Telraam's citizen window sensors add pedestrian and cyclist counts on residential streets at 15-minute resolution through an open API, with roughly 2,000 devices across 19 countries, though its licence is reported inconsistently as CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0 and coverage clusters in Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, the UK and France ([What is Telraam](https://telraam.net/en/what-is-telraam); [Traffic Technology Today](https://www.traffictechnologytoday.com/news/traffic-counting-categorization/telraam-launches-new-ai-traffic-counting-device.html); [telraam-data PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/telraam-data/)). The gap is spatial: counters exist at tens of points per city, so they can validate a model but cannot score arbitrary neighbourhoods. Portugal is a weak case: Lisbon's open data covers the cycle network and GIRA bike-share station status, but cyclist counts exist only as PDF reports from manual counts at 60 locations, and Porto has nothing ([dados.gov.pt Rede Ciclável](https://dados.gov.pt/en/datasets/rede-ciclavel/); [CML-Ativos report 2020](https://lisboaparapessoas.pt/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/relatoriocontagensciclistas2020.pdf)).

What *is* available everywhere is the supply side: the infrastructure and environment that make a place runnable and liveable. OpenStreetMap under ODbL permits copying and adapting with attribution and share-alike on derived databases ([openstreetmap.org/copyright](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright)); Geofabrik ships daily per-country extracts ([download.geofabrik.de](https://download.geofabrik.de/)); OSMnx (MIT) models walking and cycling networks, amenities, green space and elevation ([osmnx.readthedocs.io](https://osmnx.readthedocs.io/en/stable/)); and Overture Maps offers the same base themes under ODbL with Places mostly under CDLA Permissive 2.0 ([Overture attribution](https://docs.overturemaps.org/attribution/)). For Europe the environmental stack is unusually rich and CC BY: Copernicus Urban Atlas land use per functional urban area under a "full, open and free access" policy ([CLMS data policy](https://land.copernicus.eu/en/data-policy); [Urban Atlas 2021](https://land.copernicus.eu/en/products/urban-atlas/urban-atlas-2021)); EEA noise contours (2022 round published March 2026) and air-quality station series under CC BY 4.0 ([EEA noise exposure](https://www.eea.europa.eu/data-and-maps/data/data-on-noise-exposure-7); [EEA legal notice](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/legal-notice)); OpenAQ's free key at 60 requests per minute ([OpenAQ rate limits](https://docs.openaq.org/using-the-api/rate-limits)); Eurostat's EU-wide 1 km census grid and JRC's GHSL population under CC BY 4.0 ([GISCO population grids](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/en/web/gisco/geodata/population-distribution/population-grids); [GHSL](https://human-settlement.emergency.copernicus.eu/GHSLhowToCite.php)); Portugal's INE 2021 grid and BGRI sub-sections, which INE's general policy places under CC BY 4.0 ([INE mapas](https://mapas.ine.pt/download/index2021.phtml); [INE CC BY wording](https://ine.pt/ine_novidades/ODS_2019/2)); and the EU Quality of Life in European Cities survey microdata under CC BY 4.0 for a perceptions layer ([DG REGIO](https://ec.europa.eu/regional_policy/information-sources/maps/quality-of-life_en)). The proprietary indices are poor backbones: Walk Score's API "is supported in the United States and Canada" only, free for basic scores and from **$115 per month** for detail ([Walk Score API](https://www.walkscore.com/professional/api.php); [Walk Score pricing](https://www.walkscore.com/professional/pricing.php)), and Numbeo's city-level survey API starts at **$260 per month** ([Numbeo API](https://www.numbeo.com/common/api.jsp)).

| Layer | Source | Licence | Cost | Role in the score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Footway/cycleway/path density, intersections, parks, amenities, slope | OSM via Geofabrik + OSMnx; Overture | ODbL (share-alike) | Free | Core supply-side components |
| Population and dwelling density | Eurostat 1 km grid, GHSL, INE BGRI, ONS OAs | CC BY 4.0 / OGL | Free | Denominator and density component |

The single licensing interaction to manage is ODbL share-alike: once OSM-derived metrics are merged into a published score database, the whole database must be offered under ODbL with "© OpenStreetMap contributors", so either keep OSM-derived layers separable or accept ODbL for the output.

## A static hex-score map costs under $12 a month to run

Hosting is not the binding cost; data and developer time are. The documented 2024–2026 low-cost stack is a batch pipeline in DuckDB with its spatial and H3 community extensions, which reads GeoParquet (including Overture data straight from object storage over httpfs), assigns each feature to an H3 cell, groups by cell with a `HAVING count >= k` suppression step, and writes GeoParquet or GeoJSON for tippecanoe ([DuckDB community extensions](https://duckdb.org/2024/07/05/community-extensions); [DuckDB spatial](https://duckdb.org/docs/extensions/spatial); [DuckDB geospatial chapter](https://cng-python.gishub.org/book/analytics/duckdb-geospatial)). Tippecanoe writes a PMTiles archive directly, a single file of tile pyramids designed for static storage, with BSD-licensed reference clients for MapLibre GL JS, Leaflet and OpenLayers ([github.com/protomaps/PMTiles](https://github.com/protomaps/PMTiles)). Protomaps recommends Cloudflare R2 because it charges no egress ([PMTiles cloud storage](https://docs.protomaps.com/pmtiles/cloud-storage)); R2's free tier covers **10 GB of storage and 10 million reads per month**, with reads at $0.36 per million beyond that ([Cloudflare R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)). Protomaps' own worked example puts a site with 625,000 sessions and 10 million tile requests per month at **$11.45 on Cloudflare versus $119.56 on AWS and $3,640 on Google Maps Dynamic Maps** ([Protomaps cost calculator](https://docs.protomaps.com/deploy/cost)), and Pinball Map's self-hosted tiles cost $1.67 in their first month ([blog.pinballmap.com](https://blog.pinballmap.com/2024/11/05/protomaps-tile-hosting/)). At the implied 16 tile requests per session, a hobby site with 10,000 monthly users generates about 160,000 reads and stays inside the free tier; the realistic recurring bill is **$0–5 per month plus a domain**, rising to about $12 only if a Cloudflare Worker is added for caching or custom routing. A PostGIS server behind Martin is unnecessary at this scale and would add VPS cost; Martin remains the right tool if per-address network catchments are needed later ([github.com/maplibre/martin](https://github.com/maplibre/martin)).

On the client, serve the hex scores as a vector layer with one feature per H3 cell carrying each component as an attribute, styled with MapLibre data-driven expressions. If users are allowed to reweight components live, deck.gl's `H3HexagonLayer` recomputes per-cell colour in JavaScript from the attribute columns without regenerating tiles ([deck.gl docs](https://deck.gl/docs/api-reference/geo-layers/h3-hexagon-layer)). Because the only temporal scale at which crowdsourced activity data has been shown to be precise is monthly or annual (Oslo: R² = 0.9 at monthly scale in summer, poor at weekly and daily scales and in winter) ([NINA Brage record](https://brage.nina.no/nina-xmlui/handle/11250/3091374)), a monthly batch job is sufficient and no live database or API is needed. Useful open-source references are erik/derive (591 stars, MIT), a browser-only personal heatmap renderer where "no data is uploaded", which is the template for a privacy-preserving contribution path ([github.com/erik/derive](https://github.com/erik/derive)); erik/hotpot, a self-hostable heatmap tile renderer for GPX/TCX/FIT ([github.com/erik/hotpot](https://github.com/erik/hotpot)); Roy et al.'s published Strava bias-correction code ([github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction](https://github.com/Avipsa1/Strava_Bias_Correction)); and the 15-minute-city score toolkit on Zenodo ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/doi/10.5281/zenodo.14231533)). Avoid forking Flomp/wanderer unless prepared to publish source, since it is AGPL-3.0 ([github.com/Flomp/wanderer](https://github.com/Flomp/wanderer)).

For the scoring method, the published indices converge on simple, explainable aggregation, and that convergence is the transparency standard to copy. Walk Score awards maximum points to amenities within a five-minute walk, applies a decay to zero at thirty minutes, and penalises low density and long blocks, but keeps its weights proprietary and patented ([Walk Score methodology](https://www.walkscore.com/methodology.shtml)); an open replication with decay 0.008 over 800 m reaches Spearman ρ ≈ 0.91 against it, so an exponential-decay amenity score over OSM or Overture POIs is a sufficient substitute ([Environment and Planning B](https://doi.org/10.1177/23998083251377116)). Bike Score uses **four equally weighted components** (bike lanes from OSM, hills, destinations and connectivity, and census bicycle-commute share mapped 0–10% to 0–100) ([Bike Score methodology](https://www.walkscore.com/bike-score-methodology.shtml)). The Australian Urban Liveability Index, computed for 1.55 million address points, normalises each indicator to mean 100 and SD 10 and subtracts a penalty for imbalance across indicators so that one strong component cannot compensate for a weak one; its authors rejected PCA and factor weighting specifically because they are hard to explain to policymakers ([PMC6558748](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558748)). Sony CSL's 15-minute-city tool ranks roughly 10,000 cities by "proximity time" from OSM data, and its sharpest critique is instructive: it "does not take into account the socioeconomic differences of urban populations", so low-density wealthy suburbs and disadvantaged peripheries score alike ([whatif.sonycsl.it](https://whatif.sonycsl.it/15mincity/); [Science Media Centre Spain](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/node/4025)). The design that follows is a per-hex composite with an explicit published formula, equal or user-adjustable weights, within-city standardisation to percentiles, an imbalance penalty, and every component exposed on click.

## Recommended path: ship an open active-living index first, earn an activity layer later

The verdict is a conditional yes. A neighbourhood map in the spirit of the LinkedIn post is feasible, but only after replacing its data source and softening its claim. Strava heat is simultaneously the legally unavailable input and the methodologically weakest one; the supply-side features that the viral screenshots actually render are the open, defensible part. The product to build is an "active-living index": how runnable, walkable, green, quiet and well-served each hexagon is, with real counts used to validate rather than to colour the map.

### Caveats that must shape the scoring, not just the footnotes

The academic record on Strava is consistent and should govern any later activity layer. Strava counts track physical counters only when Strava's share of total traffic is high enough: Fischer, Nelson and Winters found correlation of **R = 0.42 in Vancouver in 2019 at a 5.7% Strava share versus 0.85–0.87 in Victoria at around 7%**, and proposed roughly 7% penetration as the floor for reliable use ([Fischer et al. 2022](https://findingspress.org/article/33280)). Roy et al. achieved R² = 0.76 against official counts in Maricopa County and predicted 86% of Tempe segments within ±100 daily cyclists, but only after a LASSO-plus-Poisson correction whose significant covariates were proportion of white population, median household income, traffic speed and distance to green space; in other words, census demographics are regressors in the bias model, not a simple per-capita denominator ([MDPI Urban Science](https://www.mdpi.com/2413-8851/3/2/62/htm)). The user base is skewed hard: one 2025 recreation study's Strava sample was **93.5% male, 93.6% cyclists and 62.9% aged 35–54** against 43.9% in the on-site survey ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213078025001549)); children and teenagers were about 23% under-represented in a 2023 comparison with field observation ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169204623000051)); and walkers and runners are captured far less than cyclists ([IDEAS/RePEc, Lee & Sener](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/transr/v41y2021i1p27-47.html)). Transport professionals interviewed for a 2026 *Mobilities* paper accept Strava Metro as statistically valid yet warn that it "disproportionally capture[s] journeys made by younger male cyclists" and that privileging the visible distorts planning toward the already served ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17450101.2025.2542163)). Even Strava's own privacy binning matters: applying its post-2018 discretisation to raw Glasgow data produced "significant amount of information loss" and could have reversed earlier conclusions ([IDEAS/RePEc, Raturi et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jotrge/v92y2021ics0966692321000739.html)). Practically: present any activity signal as a within-city percentile rather than a count, never below monthly aggregation, capped as a 0–100 sub-score in the manner of Bike Score's commute-share component, and never as the headline.

The equity and framing risk is as real as the statistical one. The 2014 Kansas City, 2015 Florida and 2019–2022 Baltimore and New York analyses all read the heatmap as a map of racial and economic segregation, and in August 2026 X users converted "wealth" to "safety" within hours and overlaid crime data; one widely liked reply noted that realtors are legally barred from discussing crime and school quality, which is exactly the context in which such a map would be used ([X post](https://x.com/NYCerInExile/status/2092008651288351088); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/strava-heatmap-running-while-black-1849677409)). A product that scores neighbourhoods will be read as a who-lives-here map whether or not that is intended. The defences are to score infrastructure and environment rather than people, to avoid "safety" and "desirability" language, to include components that favour under-served areas (noise, heat exposure, missing sidewalks) so the index points at deficits rather than ratifying privilege, and to publish the formula.

### Privacy design if any traces are ever ingested

Aggregated location data is not automatically anonymous. The EDPB's 2020 guidelines state that location data are "notoriously difficult to anonymise", that mobility traces "are inherently highly correlated and unique", that this "also holds for location data that is poorly aggregated", that anonymisation requires "a reasonably large set of individuals" and that transparency about the method is "highly encouraged" ([EDPB Guidelines 04/2020](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_20200420_contact_tracing_covid_with_annex_en.pdf)). The concrete attack is documented: NC State researchers crawled Strava's aggregated heatmap, detected heat "notches" entering residential parcels and matched them to voter rolls, finding **17 of 20 manually checked matches showed a clear notch and a 31.7–37.5% chance of locating a given user's home**, with sparse areas most exposed and dense areas protected by volume ([ConPro '23 PDF](https://conpro23.ieee-security.org/papers/childs-conpro23.pdf)). The same mechanism exposed military bases in 2018, the movements of Macron, Biden and Putin via bodyguards' accounts in 2024, and the Swedish prime minister's home and travel in 2025 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/01/28/global-heat-map-for-joggers-exposing-sensitive-us-military-information.html); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/10/29/biden-trump-harris-confidential-movements-fitness-app-strava-le-monde); [The Local](https://www.thelocal.se/20250708/sapo-bodyguards-strava-runs-reveal-locations-of-swedish-pm-and-royals)). The controls follow directly: a minimum of distinct contributors per cell (Strava's reported ≥3 users per segment is the industry floor, and sparse areas need more), coarser H3 resolution or a zoom cap where density is low, suppression of heat within a buffer of residential footprints or snapping to street centrelines as the ConPro authors propose, and a published anonymisation method. If users upload GPX files, the site becomes a GDPR controller for raw traces with all that implies; processing in the browser only, as dérive does, and storing nothing but the aggregated cell counts is the cleanest design. Rendering a crowdsourced "heat" layer also brushes against the heatmap patents Strava asserted against Garmin; the suit was dropped without prejudice and commentators expected prior-art challenges, but it is a risk worth a legal opinion before launch ([BikeRadar](https://www.bikeradar.com/news/strava-sues-garmin-over-segments-and-heatmaps)).

### A three-stage build

Stage one is a single-city pilot on supply-side open data, shipped as a static site: Geofabrik extract, OSMnx network and amenity metrics per H3 cell at resolution 8 or 9, census grid for density, Urban Atlas or NDVI for green space, EEA contours for noise, slope from a DEM, standardised to within-city percentiles with an imbalance penalty and an exposed formula, written to PMTiles on R2 and rendered in MapLibre. If the pilot city is Lisbon, every layer listed above is available; what is missing is any open counter feed, so validation there would rely on GIRA bike-share occupancy as a weak proxy ([dados.gov.pt GIRA](https://dados.gov.pt/en/datasets/gira-bicicletas-de-lisboa)). A second pilot in a counter-rich city such as Paris or Dublin would allow stage two: regress the index components against counter volumes, publish the fit, let users reweight components, and expand city by city while harvesting Opendatasoft, Socrata and CKAN counter feeds, which share standard APIs. Stage three, only if there is an audience, adds a consented contribution layer processed client-side from users' own exports (Strava's bulk export remains free and outside the API Agreement under Policy §6.6, as do Garmin and Polar exports), published only above the contributor threshold and at coarsened resolution ([API Policy](https://www.strava.com/legal/api_policy)). A parallel, non-product route to a Strava validation is an academic partnership: Metro's academic programme admits accredited researchers for one year of non-commercial access, with 2027 cohort applications closing 23 October 2026, and a published validation paper could legitimately report how well the open index tracks Strava volumes even though the data itself could never flow into the site ([Metro Academics](https://metro.strava.com/academics)).

On sustainability, the comparable indie sites survive on cheap annual tiers (VeloViewer PRO at £10 a year and PRO+ at £20) and feature gating, with no documented case of an activity layer licensed to a property portal ([blog.veloviewer.com](https://blog.veloviewer.com/?p=2237)). The one precedent for portal adoption is Walk Score, picked up by Zillow and others in January 2009 across more than 80 million listings because it was address-level, nationally complete and explainable in a sentence ([Inman News](https://www.inman.com/2009/01/08/listings-get-walkability-ratings/)). An open, Europe-wide, address-level active-living score has no incumbent and could meet all three criteria; a Strava-derived heat score, sparse and bias-laden, could not.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that the heatmap's appeal and its unavailability are the same fact. Strava's data is compelling because it is behavioural, and it is locked because behavioural location data is both commercially precious to a company heading for an IPO and dangerous to the people it describes. Every actor who has tried to repurpose it publicly, from VeloViewer and CityStrides to Hoodmaps, has been pushed back within weeks, and the 2026 legal texts now anticipate the AI-era workarounds too. A developer who starts from "how do I get the heat" will spend the project fighting cookies and clauses; one who starts from "what does the heat actually trace" discovers that parks, waterfronts, trails, slope, continuous footways, noise and density are open, global and licence-clean, and that the residual the heatmap adds on top is mostly the demographic skew everyone warns about.

The opportunity, therefore, is not a Strava mirror but the thing nobody has shipped: an open, transparent, Europe-first Walk Score with running and cycling in mind, validated against public counters and honest about what it cannot see. That product is buildable by one person for the price of a domain, carries no platform dependency, and leaves a door open for consented crowd data later without ever asking Strava's permission. If the heatmap trend has a durable lesson, it is that people want a single map that tells them where everyday active life is easy; the data to draw that map already exists in the commons.
